<div class="speaker-bio" style="display:none;"><p>Raja Mukerji is co-founder and Chief Scientist at ExtraHop, where he helped create the network detection and response category now used to defend hospitals, banks, and critical infrastructure worldwide. Before ExtraHop, he co-created TMOS, the operating system behind F5’s application delivery platform. He holds a computer engineering degree from MSOE, where he was named to the Wall of Distinction in 2025. He writes and speaks on AI, security, and what it means to build for the long game.</p></div>
<div class="speaker-abstract" style="display:none;"><p>Artificial intelligence will win every finite game we point it at. Bounded problems, defined objectives, measurable outcomes: machines are already better than us at most of these, and getting better faster than our institutions are adjusting. That’s the source of the anxiety, and it’s not misplaced. But it obscures the more interesting fact. A system that optimizes perfectly still has no reason to. It cannot want anything. Every objective it pursues was chosen by someone, for reasons the machine has no access to. What we’ve automated is the answering. The asking is still entirely ours, and we’ve spent remarkably little time getting good at it.</p>
<p>Drawing on a career spent building infrastructure, from co-creating the operating system behind F5’s application delivery platform to co-founding ExtraHop and creating the network detection and response category, Raja Mukerji argues that the useful question isn’t which jobs survive, but what we organize around when optimization becomes free. Ikigai and James Carse’s distinction between finite and infinite games offer a more honest answer than most of the current discourse: what remains ours is not the capacity to solve defined problems but the capacity to decide which are worth solving, and to keep playing games that have no win condition. The communities best positioned for what’s coming aren’t the ones optimizing fastest. They’re the ones that know why they’re playing.</p>
<p>Mukerji left MSOE in 1998 with a computer engineering degree and the industry’s standing assumption that the consequential work happened somewhere else. The assumption was wrong. The technology he and his co-founder built now sits underneath hospitals, banks, and critical infrastructure around the world, a category that didn’t exist until they made it.</p></div>

    <div class="speaker-abstract" style="display:none;">
        <p>This talk is about the evolution of using LLMs for real-world document understanding and structured extraction. Rather than focusing on a single method, it traces how our approach changed over time as model capabilities improved and new operational constraints emerged.</p>
        
        <p>We began with an OCR-first pipeline that extracted text, key-value pairs, checkboxes, and confidence scores, then used an LLM to interpret that structured output. That gave us a useful confidence signal for routing uncertain cases to human review, but it also created new problems: brittle OCR outputs and weak reasoning between extracted text and document images.</p>
        
        <p>As multimodal models improved, we shifted to using the model itself for both reading and extraction, which simplified the system and improved performance. But this also removed the confidence layer we had relied on. To recover it, we used a simple but surprisingly effective ensemble strategy: run the model multiple times with higher stochasticity and use agreement across runs as a proxy for confidence.</p>
        
        <p>The talk closes with the next open challenge: how to keep the benefits of multi-pass confidence estimation without paying for multiple calls on every document. It’s a story about how practical AI systems evolve through iteration, tradeoffs, and a few useful hacks.</p>
